# Behavioural Question Bank

How to use: answer each question aloud in under two minutes using a structure — Situation, Action, Result, what you Learned (SARL) — then compare with the model answer, which gives the structure of a strong response and the specific things Glencore, Trafigura, Vitol, Mercuria and Gunvor listen for: ownership (you did it, not "the team"), commercial instinct (you can see where the money is), numeracy (you quantify without being asked), staying calm under pressure, low ego (you can take a blunt correction and move on), and hunger (you want the desk, the travel, the hours). Prepare three or four real stories that can be re-cut for several questions; interviewers ask for specifics and will dig. Anything about company results, settlements or strategy should be checked against the latest brief before the interview.

Coverage:
- Motivation: why physical trading, why Glencore versus Trafigura/Vitol, why not a bank or hedge fund
- Fit: Marketing vs Industrial, which desk, first 90 days, five-year view
- Character: risk appetite, being wrong, blunt bosses, low ego, working across time zones and relocating
- Pressure: teamwork under pressure, counterparty disputes, supplier default
- Ethics: compliance dilemmas, and how to discuss Glencore's 2022 settlements if raised
- Commercial evidence: a deal or negotiation you have done, how you read and form a view
- Closing: questions to ask the interviewer

---

### Q1. Walk me through your CV in two minutes.
**Difficulty:** 1
**Model answer:**
- Structure: a one-line headline (who you are and what you want), then three chapters — education, relevant experience, why commodities now — each with one concrete number or achievement, then a single sentence on why this house.
- Lead with evidence of the traits they hire for: numeracy (a quantitative degree or a project with real numbers), ownership (something you ran end to end), commercial exposure (a sale, a negotiation, a P&L), resilience (something hard you finished).
- Cut anything that does not serve the story; a trader will interrupt at 90 seconds if you are still on school.
- Signal you understand the job: "I want to be on a desk where the P&L comes from moving physical product and managing spreads, not from calling flat price."
- Finish with a hook the interviewer can pick up: a deal you did, a market you follow closely, a language you speak, a place you have worked.
- Do not read the CV back to them; they have it. Tell them what is not on it.
**Pushback:**
- "Why did you leave [X] after a year?" (Own the decision; don't blame people.)
- "What's the one number on your CV you're proudest of?" (Have one; know how it was calculated.)
**Red flags:** Chronological recitation, no numbers, no reason for commodities beyond "it's exciting".

### Q2. Why physical commodities trading?
**Difficulty:** 1
**Model answer:**
- Give a real origin: a moment where you saw the physical world price itself — a shipping delay, an energy bill, a mine visit, an internship watching a cargo priced — and explain that the appeal is that the edge comes from logistics, information and execution rather than from a screen.
- Show you understand what the job is: arbitrage of place, time and quality; managing counterparties, ships, tanks and hedges; long hours and constant problem-solving; P&L that is measured daily.
- Show you understand what it is not: it is not a hedge fund; flat price is hedged; the trainee years are operations, contracts, and learning the flows — and you want that.
- Connect to your traits: comfortable with numbers and ambiguity, like ownership, like being on the phone, want to travel and see the assets.
- Mention what you have done to test the interest (reading, following spreads in the brief, talking to people in the industry, a trading simulation) — hunger shows in preparation.
- One sentence on the long game: the ambition is to run a book, and the path is operations → junior trading → book.
**Pushback:**
- "It's mostly operations for the first two years. Still want it?" (Yes, and explain why ops is where you learn what actually makes money — demurrage, quality claims, laytime.)
- "What would make you leave?" (Be honest but show you're not a flight risk: only if you stopped learning.)
**Red flags:** "I love markets and want to make money fast," or a description that sounds like a macro hedge fund.

### Q3. Why Glencore rather than Trafigura or Vitol?
**Difficulty:** 1
**Model answer:**
- Know the real differences. Glencore: the only one of the big houses that is a listed, integrated miner-trader; Marketing is fed by Industrial assets (copper, cobalt, zinc, nickel, coal, ferroalloys) plus third-party oil, gas, agri (via Viterra historically — check the current status) — so a trainee sees mine-to-market. Trafigura: private partnership, oil and metals concentrates, heavy in logistics assets (Puma, Impala, Nyrstar); Vitol: the largest independent oil trader, private, oil-and-gas-centric, refining and power; Mercuria/Gunvor: smaller, oil-and-gas-led with growing power/metals.
- Pick a reason that only Glencore satisfies: e.g. "I want to be in metals, and the integration with the mines gives Marketing a flow and an information edge nobody else has," or "I want to be where coal, copper and cobalt intersect with the transition debate, and Glencore is the place that debate is being decided."
- Show awareness of listed-company reality: transparency, capital discipline, the remediation post-2022, and the fact that graduate programmes there are structured.
- Avoid saying anything untrue about competitors; a Glencore trader will have friends at Trafigura.
- Close with a personal link if you have one: a person you met, a desk you spoke to, a location (Baar, London, Singapore) that fits your life.
**Pushback:**
- "Trafigura offered you more money. What now?" (Stick to the reason; if the reason is money, they will know.)
- "Which desk at Glencore and why not the others?" (Have a first and second choice and a reason grounded in the flows.)
**Red flags:** "Glencore is the biggest" (depends on the metric), or generic praise that would apply to any house.

### Q4. Why not a bank or a hedge fund?
**Difficulty:** 1
**Model answer:**
- Bank commodities desks are client-facing and capital-constrained; you make markets and structure hedges for producers and consumers — valuable, but you are not the one owning the cargo, the tank or the mine, and the physical business has largely left the banks.
- Hedge funds trade paper on a view; the edge is analysis and timing, the feedback is fast, but there is no logistics, no counterparties, no ships and no operational optionality. A physical trader's edge is in doing the difficult things — moving product where it is hard to move.
- Say what you want that only physical gives: ownership of a full chain (buy, ship, hedge, deliver), a career that builds relationships with producers and refiners, and P&L that comes from problems solved rather than from being right about the Fed.
- Be honest about trade-offs: less glamour, more operations early, travel to difficult places, but a stronger and more durable skill set.
- Show you know traders hedge: "I'm not looking for directional risk; I'm looking to learn how spreads, basis and freight are managed."
**Pushback:**
- "Hedge funds pay more and you'd be trading from day one. Why not?" (Because the skill I want compounds slower but lasts longer; and the trading houses' seat gives information a fund cannot buy.)
- "Would you consider a bank's physical arm?" (Be aware some banks still have physical capability; answer on the merits.)
**Red flags:** Criticises banks or funds as "boring" without understanding either.

### Q5. Glencore has Marketing and Industrial. Where do you fit, and what's the difference in what they value?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Industrial: the mines, smelters and refineries — copper, cobalt, zinc, lead, nickel, ferroalloys, coal (thermal and steelmaking). Valued: operational discipline, cost control, safety, engineering and project skills, long time horizons; earnings move with price and volume guidance.
- Marketing: buying, selling, blending, storing, financing and shipping the commodities — Glencore's own production plus a large third-party book, across metals, energy (oil, gas, LNG, coal marketing) and, historically, agriculture through Viterra. Valued: commercial instinct, numeracy, speed, relationships, calm under pressure. Guided as a long-term EBIT range that is meant to be repeatable across the cycle (check the latest).
- Where you fit: say Marketing if you want to trade, and be specific about desk (copper concentrates, zinc, coal, oil products, LNG) and why (your background, languages, the flows you find interesting).
- Show you understand the link: Marketing gets captive flow and information from Industrial; Industrial gets a marketing arm that can blend, finance and place product. A trainee who understands both is more useful.
- Mention the trainee reality: rotations through operations, finance, risk and the desk; the trader is the destination, not the start.
**Pushback:**
- "Would you take an Industrial role if Marketing didn't come through?" (Honest answer with a reason; a rigid "no" looks like you don't understand the company.)
- "Which is riskier for the group?" (Industrial carries capex and price risk; Marketing carries counterparty, operational and compliance risk; different risks, not more or less.)
**Red flags:** Doesn't know Glencore owns mines, or thinks Marketing is only paper trading.

### Q6. Which commodity desk do you want, and why that one and not the others?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Name one, with a second choice. Justify it with the structure of the market: e.g. copper concentrates (TC/RCs, payables, smelter relationships, Chinese demand, a market Glencore leads), zinc (a metal with real blending and smelter economics), coal (Glencore's largest seaborne franchise, quality blending, Asia demand), oil products (arb-driven, freight-intensive), or LNG (destination optionality and hub spreads).
- Show you know what the daily job is on that desk: who the counterparties are, what index it prices off, what the key spreads are, what the logistics look like.
- Connect to yourself: languages (Mandarin for metals into China, Spanish for Latin American concentrates and Colombian coal, French for the DRC and West Africa), engineering or chemistry background (quality and blending), a geography you know.
- Be flexible: "If the firm sees me somewhere else, I'll go where the flow is; the skills transfer."
- Avoid oil-by-default if you cannot explain why oil over metals at a house whose core is metals and coal.
**Pushback:**
- "That desk has no openings. What now?" (Second choice, with a reason, and a statement that you'll make yourself useful anywhere.)
- "What's the most important number on that desk this week?" (From the brief: a spread, a TC, a freight rate; have one.)
**Red flags:** "I'm open to anything" with no preference, or a desk choice with no market knowledge behind it.

### Q7. What's your risk appetite? Give me a concrete example.
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- The right posture: high appetite for calculated risk with a defined downside; zero appetite for unbounded, unmeasured, or reputational risk. Say that in one sentence.
- Situation: a decision where you put something at stake — money, a grade, a job outcome, a reputation — with a quantified downside and upside.
- Action: how you sized it (what could I lose, what was the probability, what was the exit), what you did to cut the tail (a fallback, a hedge, a deadline), and how you monitored it.
- Result: what happened, in numbers. It is fine if it lost money as long as the sizing was right.
- Learned: the difference between risk you are paid for and risk you are not; that limits exist to protect the franchise; that you write the exit before you enter.
- Connect to the desk: a trader is not paid for taking risk, they are paid for managing it — the cargo must move, but the flat price is hedged and the spread is sized to VaR.
**Pushback:**
- "Give me an example where you took too much risk." (Have one; show what you changed.)
- "Would you breach a limit if you were sure you were right?" (No. Escalate. Being sure is not a control.)
**Red flags:** "I'm a big risk taker" with a story about gambling, or no example at all.

### Q8. Tell me about a time you were wrong. What did you change?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Pick a real error with consequences, ideally analytical (a view, a forecast, a decision) rather than a typo. Traders respect people who have been wrong properly.
- Situation: what you believed and why it seemed reasonable at the time; the evidence you weighted.
- Action: when you realised, how quickly you acted (cut the position, told the person, changed the plan) — speed of admission is the trait being tested.
- Result: the cost, quantified; how it was contained.
- Learned: a specific process change — pre-mortems, writing the invalidation condition before entering, checking a second source, sizing smaller when conviction comes from a narrative rather than data.
- End with how you would apply it on a desk: "I write down what would make me wrong before I put on a spread; if it happens, I'm out, no debate."
**Pushback:**
- "Were you wrong or unlucky?" (Distinguish process from outcome; show you can tell the difference honestly.)
- "Who did you tell and when?" (Immediately, and up the chain; concealment is a firing offence in this industry.)
**Red flags:** A humble-brag ("I worked too hard"), or blaming others.

### Q9. Describe a time you worked in a team under real pressure.
**Difficulty:** 1
**Model answer:**
- Situation: a deadline or crisis with a real cost of failure, with clear stakes (money, safety, a client).
- Action: what you specifically did — took a role, allocated tasks, kept a list, communicated status, made the call nobody wanted to make, or did the unglamorous job. Show low ego: you did what was needed, not what was visible.
- Result: the outcome, with numbers if possible, and how the team came out of it.
- Learned: pressure exposes communication gaps; the fix is short, frequent updates and an explicit owner for each item — exactly what happens on a desk when a vessel is delayed and three people need the same information.
- Show you can stay calm: name the moment you felt the pressure and what you did with it (slowed down, wrote it down, called someone).
- Trading houses run small teams in which operators, traders and finance depend on each other minute to minute; they are listening for someone who communicates upward early and doesn't hide problems.
**Pushback:**
- "What did you do badly in that situation?" (Have an answer; it makes the rest credible.)
- "How would you handle the same thing if the person under pressure was your boss?" (Support, give facts, don't add noise.)
**Red flags:** "We" for everything, no personal contribution, no conflict.

### Q10. A counterparty disputes a cargo's quality on discharge and refuses to pay, or a supplier defaults on a delivery you have already sold. What do you do?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- First hour: protect the position and the cash. For a default, cover the sale — buy replacement product in the market and hedge the price gap — before arguing about who pays. For a quality dispute, get the independent inspector's certificates (load and discharge), the contract's spec clause and tolerance, and the retained samples.
- Read the contract: Incoterms, quality determination clause (whose inspector is final and binding?), claims windows, force majeure, dispute resolution (arbitration seat, governing law).
- Escalate immediately: trader, operations, legal, credit and the desk head all need to know the same facts; write a one-page timeline.
- Commercial approach: preserve the relationship where it has value — a settlement (a price adjustment, a replacement cargo, an offset against future deals) is often better than arbitration; but do not concede a claim without evidence.
- Quantify: the exposure (cargo value, replacement cost difference, demurrage, hedging losses), the recovery chances, and the credit reserve.
- Learned: check counterparty credit and performance history before you deal; structure with letters of credit, prepayment or parent guarantees when performance risk is high; and keep the sample chain intact — most disputes are decided on paperwork.
**Pushback:**
- "The counterparty is a strategic supplier in a country we can't afford to lose. Now what?" (Settle commercially, but document, and make the commercial concession visible and approved, not buried.)
- "Who signs off a settlement above $1 million?" (Not you — know that authority limits exist.)
**Red flags:** Starts with "I'd call my lawyer" or has no idea that inspectors and samples exist.

### Q11. You notice a colleague booking a trade with a counterparty whose ownership is unclear, in a jurisdiction where agents are common. What do you do?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Principle: in this industry, KYC, sanctions screening and third-party due diligence are not optional; bribery through intermediaries is exactly what the 2022 cases were about. The answer is to stop and escalate, not to "have a word".
- Action: pause the trade if it is within your control; raise it with the colleague directly ("Has compliance cleared this counterparty?"); if the answer is unsatisfactory, go to compliance or your manager the same day. Use the formal channels, in writing.
- Show you know the tools: KYC on beneficial ownership, sanctions lists, politically exposed persons checks, third-party intermediary approval processes, and the speak-up policy.
- Be measured about the colleague: it may be an oversight; escalation is protective for them too.
- Show the commercial understanding: losing one trade costs a margin; a compliance breach costs the licence to operate, the bank lines and the share price.
- Learned/applied: you would rather be the person who slowed a deal than the person who was silent.
**Pushback:**
- "Your boss says 'it's fine, we've dealt with them for years'." (Ask for the compliance approval reference; if it doesn't exist, escalate above the boss.)
- "Would you report a friend?" (Yes; the friend's interest is served by early escalation too.)
**Red flags:** "I'd stay out of it" or any hint that commercial pressure justifies skipping checks.

### Q12. A trader on the panel says: "You've read about the 2022 settlements. What do you think of Glencore's ethics?"
**Difficulty:** 3
**Model answer:**
- Acknowledge directly: in 2022 Glencore resolved bribery and market-manipulation investigations with the US DOJ and CFTC, the UK SFO and Brazilian authorities, paying roughly $1.5 billion in total; conduct included bribery via intermediaries in several African and Latin American countries and manipulation of oil benchmark assessments. Don't soften it.
- Show you know the remediation: guilty pleas and acceptance of responsibility; an independent compliance monitor for several years; a rebuilt ethics and compliance programme; changes to leadership and board; restrictions on the use of intermediaries; enhanced training and speak-up channels; ongoing regulatory attention in other jurisdictions (check the latest brief for status).
- Give your judgement in one sentence: "It was serious and the company says so; the test is whether the controls work now, and my expectation is that they are tighter than most of the industry because they had to be."
- Don't lecture: the person opposite may have lived through it; keep it factual and forward-looking.
- Turn it into a question if invited: "How has it changed how a deal gets approved on your desk?" — shows interest, not judgement.
- The trait tested: can you talk about something uncomfortable calmly, accurately, without either defensiveness or moralising.
**Pushback:**
- "Would you have done what those traders did if it was the only way to get the deal?" (No — and the reason is commercial as well as ethical: the deal wasn't worth the franchise.)
- "Do you think it still happens?" (Say you can't know from outside, but the monitor and the controls exist because it must not; you'd escalate if you saw it.)
**Red flags:** Doesn't know the facts, dismisses it as "everyone did it", or moralises at the interviewer.

### Q13. The job may require relocating to Baar, Singapore, Johannesburg or a mine site, and working across time zones. How do you feel about that?
**Difficulty:** 1
**Model answer:**
- Say yes with evidence: a time you moved, lived abroad, worked across time zones or in a place that was not comfortable. Traders want people who are mobile in fact, not in theory.
- Show you understand the daily rhythm: an oil or metals desk in Europe catches Asia in the morning and the US in the afternoon; calls at 06:00 and 22:00 are normal; the LME ring and Asian physical windows do not move for you.
- Name constraints honestly if you have them (a partner, a visa) and the plan for them; vagueness reads as risk.
- Mention the assets: seeing a mine, a smelter, a terminal is part of learning the business; you would go.
- Keep it short; enthusiasm without specifics sounds hollow.
**Pushback:**
- "Would you take a two-year posting in a hardship location?" (Answer honestly; a "yes" you cannot deliver is worse than a qualified answer.)
- "How do you keep sharp when you're on calls at both ends of the day?" (Routine, sleep discipline, and knowing when you're not sharp enough to size a trade.)
**Red flags:** Hesitation, or a preference for London only.

### Q14. What do you read every day, and how do you form a view?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Daily inputs: price and curve data (spreads, not just flat), an inventory/flow source (EIA weekly, OPEC/IEA monthly, LME stock reports, ship tracking summaries), a physical-market price reporter (Platts, Argus, Fastmarkets), one general financial source, and the brief in this repo.
- Process: start from the curve and spreads (what is the market saying?), then the physical (are differentials and premiums confirming?), then the narrative (what is the news claiming, and is it in the price?). Write a one-line view with two drivers and one invalidation condition.
- Weekly: check positioning (COT), refinery margins, freight, and the big scheduled events (OPEC+, central banks, Chinese data).
- Show a specific example from the brief: "This morning's M1-M2 and DFL disagree, which makes me think the backwardation is paper-driven."
- Emphasise talking to people: brokers, operators, a friend at a refiner; physical trading rewards the phone.
- Admit what you don't have access to yet (proprietary flow data) and that the desk will improve the process.
**Pushback:**
- "Give me your view on one thing you read this morning." (Have it ready; use the brief.)
- "How do you avoid confirmation bias?" (Write the bear case yourself; find who is on the other side and why.)
**Red flags:** "I read the FT" and nothing quantitative, or cannot produce a view on the spot.

### Q15. Tell me about a deal or negotiation you have done.
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Any real negotiation counts: a sale, a supplier contract, a rent, a salary, a partnership, a fundraising, a sports transfer — as long as you can quantify it.
- Situation: what was at stake, the counterparty's interests, your BATNA and theirs (show you thought about their walk-away).
- Action: preparation (numbers, alternatives), the concessions you traded (not gave), how you created value (bundling, timing, non-price terms), and one moment where you held your ground.
- Result: the outcome in numbers versus the opening positions and versus your target.
- Learned: value is often in non-price terms (payment terms, quantity tolerance, timing) — exactly how physical contracts are negotiated; and that relationships matter because you will trade with the same counterparties for decades.
- Show low ego: you can be firm and still leave the other side feeling fairly treated.
**Pushback:**
- "What would you do differently?" (Something concrete: better anchor, more information, walked away sooner.)
- "Negotiate with me: I'm selling you a cargo, open." (Be ready to role-play; ask questions before naming a price.)
**Red flags:** No numbers, or a story where the other side simply capitulated.

### Q16. What would you do in your first 90 days on a desk?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Days 1-30: learn the flows. Sit with operations — nominations, vessel fixtures, inspectors, laytime, demurrage, documents (B/L, LC). Map the book: every open contract, its pricing basis, its hedge, its counterparty. Learn the marks and the P&L explain.
- Days 31-60: make yourself useful with numbers. Build or improve a tool the desk actually needs (freight calculator, arb landed-cost model, exposure report). Start following one spread or differential daily and write a line on it for the trader.
- Days 61-90: earn a small responsibility — a piece of scheduling, a counterparty relationship, a hedge execution under supervision — and get it right every time.
- Throughout: ask questions early in the day, not when the trader is pricing; write things down; never hide a mistake; learn who does what in risk, credit, compliance and finance.
- Success metric: the trader trusts you with a task without checking it, and you can explain yesterday's P&L.
- Show you know the culture: low ego, no politics, fast feedback; you'll get told bluntly when you're wrong and you'll say thank you.
**Pushback:**
- "What if nobody has time to teach you?" (Learn from the operators and the documents; make yourself the person who knows where everything is.)
- "What's the most common trainee mistake?" (Rushing to have views before understanding the flows; or an operational error — a wrong nomination — that costs real money.)
**Red flags:** "I'd start trading" or an answer with no operations content.

### Q17. Your boss is blunt to the point of rudeness. How do you handle it?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Separate content from tone: bluntness on a desk is usually a speed signal, not a personal one. The information in the criticism is what matters; take it, fix it, move on.
- Action: acknowledge in one sentence, ask a clarifying question if needed, fix it, and report back; don't defend, don't sulk, don't escalate over tone alone.
- Example: a time you received harsh feedback, what you did with it and the result.
- Boundaries: distinguish blunt from abusive or discriminatory; the latter is an HR and compliance matter, and you would raise it. Say that plainly.
- Show low ego and durability: you're there to learn the business, and the fastest teachers are often the least polite.
- Show self-awareness: over time, you'll learn the boss's rhythm — when to ask, when to wait — and you'll bring solutions, not problems.
**Pushback:**
- "He tells you your idea is stupid in front of the desk." (Ask what was wrong with it, learn, and if it wasn't stupid, show the numbers later, privately.)
- "Would you give blunt feedback yourself?" (Yes, with numbers, in private where possible.)
**Red flags:** Sensitivity, or claiming you'd "confront" the boss about their manner.

### Q18. What's your biggest weakness, and what have you done about it?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Pick a real one that is not disqualifying on a desk: impatience with process, a tendency to over-analyse before acting, discomfort with unstructured phone conversations, a habit of working alone.
- Evidence: a specific situation where it cost you something, quantified if possible.
- Fix: a concrete practice you have adopted (checklists, deadlines for decisions, deliberate practice on the phone, seeking feedback weekly) and evidence it is working.
- Ownership: no blaming the environment; no "perfectionism".
- Relate to the desk: say what the weakness would look like in the job and how you'd guard against it (e.g. asking for explicit deadlines from the trader).
**Pushback:**
- "That's not a weakness. Give me a real one." (Have a second, more uncomfortable one ready.)
- "How would your last manager describe your weakness?" (Same answer, or explain the difference.)
**Red flags:** A strength in disguise, or "I don't really have one".

### Q19. Where do you see yourself in five years, and what happens if you don't make trader?
**Difficulty:** 2
**Model answer:**
- Five years: running a small book or a defined piece of a desk's flow (a region, a product), trusted with counterparties and hedges, ideally after a posting abroad. Be specific about the desk you named earlier.
- Path: operations → junior trading → book; acknowledge it is not guaranteed and depends on the firm's needs and on your performance.
- If not trader: say honestly what else in the house would interest you — operations leadership, structured finance/prepayments, risk, chartering, origination on the Industrial side — and why those are real careers, not consolation prizes. The house wants people who stay.
- Show hunger without entitlement: "I'll earn the seat; if the feedback is that I'm better placed elsewhere, I'd rather hear it early."
- Show loyalty is earned both ways: you'll commit if the firm invests in you.
**Pushback:**
- "Most trainees don't become traders. Why are you different?" (Evidence, not adjectives: numeracy, a deal, a demonstrated appetite for the unglamorous work.)
- "Would you leave for a competitor's trading seat?" (Honest, but state what would keep you.)
**Red flags:** "Running my own fund", or no plan B.

### Q20. What questions do you have for us?
**Difficulty:** 1
**Model answer:**
- Ask three, ordered by how much they reveal about you: one commercial (about the desk's flow, a spread, a market — using the brief), one about how trainees are developed and evaluated (what separates the trainees who make it), one about the interviewer's own path.
- Good examples: "How has the desk's approach to counterparty and intermediary approval changed since 2022?"; "Where does the desk's edge come from — logistics, financing or information?"; "What's the trade or situation this year that taught the team the most?"; "What does a trainee need to have done by month twelve for you to consider them on track?"
- Avoid: salary, holidays, hours (you know the hours), or anything on the website.
- Listen to the answers and follow up; the exchange is the last impression.
- If a question is answered with something you disagree with, it is fine to say so politely with a reason — they are hiring for judgement.
**Pushback:**
- "You've asked about culture. What have you heard about our culture that worries you?" (Be honest but specific: hours, bluntness, the 2022 history — and what you've concluded.)
- "Any questions that would make you turn down an offer?" (Have a genuine one: role clarity, location, development path.)
**Red flags:** "No, you've covered everything," or questions that show no preparation.
